## 1. What Is Research? Core Definitions

- **Research** = "Re" + "Search" — a careful, systematic, patient study/investigation undertaken to establish facts or principles, or to create new knowledge / apply existing knowledge in new, creative ways.
- Research is a **creative process** — not pure logic. It requires imagination, initiative, intuition, curiosity ("the logic we can teach; the art we cannot").
- **Ladd's (1987) ways to foster creativity in research:** gather/use prior knowledge; exchange ideas; apply deductive logic; look at things in alternate ways; question assumptions; search for patterns/relationships; take risks; cultivate tolerance for uncertainty; allow curiosity to grow; set problems aside and return; write down thoughts; allow freedom from distraction.

### Methodology vs Method (important distinction, frequently tested)
| Method | Methodology |
|---|---|
| The specific techniques, tools and procedures used to solve the given research problem (e.g., regression analysis, surveys, experiments) | The rigorous **study of the general approach to inquiry** in a field — ensures conclusions are valid, reliable, credible |
| Covers various investigation techniques | Is the entire strategy/guidebook towards achieving the research objective |

**Research Methodology** = "the science of systematically solving a research problem" — the guidebook of research; a science in itself.

## 2. Types / Classifications of Research (as taught)

The course teaches multiple overlapping classification schemes:

**(A) By nature of information:**
- **Qualitative Research** — understands the nature/quality of phenomena; answers "WH" questions (what, why, how).
- **Quantitative Research** — understands magnitude/association; numeric data.

**(B) By utility/nature of subject matter:**
- **Basic/Fundamental/Pure/Theoretical Research** — universal utility (e.g., scientific theories).
- **Experimental or Applied Research** — limited/practical utility.

**(C) By approach:**
- **Longitudinal Research** — historical, case study, genetic research; follows same sample over time.
- **Cross-Sectional Research** — experimental, survey research; fresh sample each time, single point in time.

**(D) By method of research (5 categories):**
1. **Philosophical Research** — purely qualitative; examines a philosophy/line of thought.
2. **Historical Research** — qualitative + quantitative; deals with past events.
3. **Survey Research** — quantitative; present events; sub-divided into explanatory, correlational, exploratory.
4. **Experimental Research** — purely quantitative; deals with future events.
5. **Case-Study Research** — deals with unusual events; can be qualitative or quantitative.

**Research Method Classification specific to Computer Science** (multiple classification schemes exist):
- **Scientific** — understanding nature
- **Engineering** — providing solutions
- **Empirical** — data-centric models
- **Analytical** — theoretical formalism
- **Computing** — hybrid methods

### Other named research method types referenced across the notes
- Exploratory research (define/identify a problem)
- Empirical research (uses empirical evidence to test feasibility)
- Constructive research (tests theories, proposes solutions to a query)
- Design Science
- Mixed Methods
- Action Research (mentioned as an "important" method to know, alongside Constructive Research and Design Science, under "Important points" in Handout 1)

### Criteria of Good Research
(a) Purpose clearly defined, common concepts used; (b) procedure described in sufficient detail to permit replication; (c) procedural design carefully planned for objectivity; (d) researcher reports flaws in design with complete frankness; (e) data analysis adequate, validity/reliability checked; (f) conclusions confined to what the data justifies; (g) greater confidence warranted if researcher has integrity/reputation.

### Research Contribution — Example Types
i. Demonstration of novel possibilities; ii. Extension to a new field; iii. Falsification (showing an accepted theory/belief is untrue or has limited generalizability); iv. Incremental improvement (outperforms earlier approaches); v. New method; vi. Novel concept.

## 3. Research Questions, Hypotheses, Aims & Objectives

- **Research Question** — a specific inquiry the research seeks to answer; usually the *first step*; guides scope, data type, and analysis type.
- **Hypothesis** — a formal statement predicting the relationship between two or more variables (more direct/closed than a research question, which is open-ended).
- **Good Research Questions must be:** Feasible (within time/effort/fund budget); Clear (well-defined key terms); Significant (scope for real contribution); Ethical (no harm to humans/environment). Also: focused on a single issue, researchable, specific enough to answer thoroughly, complex enough to sustain a thesis/paper, relevant to the field.
- **Developing a research question (process):** choose broad topic → preliminary reading on debates/issues → narrow to a niche → identify a practical/theoretical problem to address.

### Aims vs Objectives
- **Aim** = what you hope to achieve (broad, statement of intent).
- **Objective** = the specific action(s) taken to achieve the aim (specific, measurable).
- **Strong verbs for objectives:** collect, construct, classify, develop, devise, measure, produce, revise, select, synthesize.
- **Weak verbs to avoid:** appreciate, consider, enquire, learn, know, understand, be aware of, listen, perceive.

### SMART Objectives Framework (explicitly taught)
- **S**pecific — clearly written, no room for confusion
- **M**easurable — create metrics to track progress
- **A**chievable — realistic given resources/budget
- **R**elevant — tied to overall research goals
- **T**ime-based — deadlines set for the whole project and sub-objectives

### General vs Specific Objectives
- **General Objectives** — state what is expected to be achieved in broad terms.
- **Specific Objectives** — broken down from general objectives; systematically address different aspects of the research problem; specify what will be carried out and why (rationale).

## 4. Research Gap — Identification Framework (Key Section)

**Definition:** A research gap is an area/topic within a field that has not been extensively researched, or a question/problem/issue not yet addressed or resolved by prior research.

### How to Identify a Research Gap (the course's explicit step-by-step process)
1. Review existing literature thoroughly.
2. Identify a specific research problem/question.
3. Analyze existing research (find unstudied areas, inconsistencies, limitations).
4. Brainstorm potential research ideas addressing the identified gaps.
5. Consult with experts to validate/refine gap ideas.
6. Refine research questions/hypotheses based on the gap.
7. Develop a research proposal outlining questions, objectives, and methods to address the gap.

> Note: identifying a research gap is explicitly called an **iterative process** requiring critical thinking and creativity; it may require revisiting/refining questions multiple times. The notes also state (Handout 4, Tutorial 4) that the typical practical order for a thesis is: **lit review → identify research gap → formulate research questions → formulate aims/objectives.**

### The 5 Types of Research Gap (this is the course's specific taxonomy — use this exact terminology)
1. **Theoretical Gap** — lack of theoretical understanding; discrepancy between existing theories and empirical evidence, or no theory explains a phenomenon.
2. **Empirical Gap** — lack of empirical evidence/data on a topic; existing research is inadequate or inconclusive.
3. **Methodological Gap** — lack of appropriate research methods/techniques to answer a question; existing methods are inadequate, outdated, or inappropriate.
4. **Practical Gap** — lack of practical application/implementation of research findings (due to financial, political, or social constraints).
5. **Knowledge Gap** — lack of knowledge/information on a topic, e.g. an emerging area or a different context/population not yet studied.

### Challenges in Identifying Gaps (acknowledged in the notes)
- Difficulty identifying gaps amid large/scattered research volume.
- Risk of oversimplification when addressing complex problems.
- Researcher bias can skew what's perceived as a "gap."
- Gap identification is inherently somewhat subjective — different researchers may disagree.

### Significance of the Study — Assessment Factors
Originality (advances knowledge/new ideas); Practical relevance (real-world implications); Theoretical contribution (new insight into frameworks); Methodological rigor (robust methods → reliable/valid data); Social/cultural impact.

## 5. Literature Review

### Definition & Purpose
A literature review is a **systematic examination of existing research** on a topic that (1) analyzes research already done, (2) identifies strengths/weaknesses, (3) identifies connections/discrepancies, (4) identifies gaps where further research is needed. It is the writing process of summarizing, synthesizing, and/or critiquing literature.

**Two key objectives:** (1) cover existing research/theories/evidence, and (2) provide the author's own critical evaluation and discussion of others' work (NOT just a chronological list/description — critique matters).

**A literature review is distinct from a research paper**: a lit review does not make a new argument from original research; it summarizes, synthesizes, critiques, and points to gaps.

### Purpose of Lit Review (as taught — 12 points across two slides)
State-of-the-art identification; understand strengths/weaknesses/controversies; link own ideas to published literature; find gaps that become research questions; provide background/context; learn methodologies used by similar research; validate originality of your work; evaluate alternative methods/models; identify methods to avoid; avoid reinventing the wheel; identify domain experts; examine validity of competing theories.

### Word Count Guidance (explicit numeric benchmark — important)
- **Annotated bibliography** entries: ~150 words per source.
- **Literature review**: typically **6,000–12,000 words** (this is the benchmark given in the course materials for a full literature review — use as reference point, though the actual TAF/thesis-specific word count should be checked against the Guidelines PDF for the current cohort's exact requirement).

### Sources: Primary, Secondary, Tertiary
- **Primary** — direct/original: peer-reviewed original research articles, conference proceedings, patents, tech/lab reports, dissertations.
- **Secondary** — indirect, reports on others' work: textbooks/monographs, review/survey papers, state-of-the-art reports, trade journal articles (non-research-based).
- **Tertiary** — reference works with agreed-upon facts, not usually author-credited: encyclopedias, dictionaries, textbooks, standards.
- **Recommended search order ("working backwards"):** Tertiary (background/definitions) → Secondary (analyses, methodologies used) → Primary (deep evidence).

### Evaluating Sources — Credibility Checklist
1. **Currency** — is the publication date sufficiently recent for the topic?
2. **Coverage/Relevance** — does it adequately cover your research need?
3. **Authority** — check author credentials, affiliations, Google Scholar h-index/citation count.
4. **Accuracy** — can claims be verified? Are sources cited?
5. **Objectivity/Purpose** — consider the author's motive and bias.

### Predatory / Blacklisted Publishers — MUST CHECK (explicitly taught)
- Check **Beall's List** of Predatory Publishers/Journals (https://beallslist.net/)
- Check **Web of Science / Clarivate Master Journal List** indexing (https://mjl.clarivate.com/home)
- Check **Scopus Indexing** via Scimago.com
- Check **Retraction Watch** (https://retractionwatch.com/) for retracted papers
- Also referenced: Scopus Discontinued List of Journals; WoS/JCR Suppression List of Journals

### Search Strategies
- **Pearl Growing / Citation Mining / Snowballing** — start from one relevant citation, dig into its reference list, then dig into references-of-references.
- **Forward Search** — find articles that cite your identified paper (use Google Scholar "cited by").
- **Backward Search** — follow the reference list of an article.
- Also: search for other papers by the same authors.
- **Databases recommended:** ACM Digital Library, ResearchGate, Google Scholar, IEEE Xplore, arXiv, DBLP.

### Organizing a Literature Review (standard 3-part structure taught)
**A. Introduction** — define/identify topic, establish reason for review, note general publication trends, explain comparison criteria.
**B. Body** — group articles into thematic clusters (chronological, thematic, or methodological grouping), proceed logically cluster-to-cluster, emphasize findings in your own words (minimize direct quotes).
**C. Conclusion** — summarize major themes/controversies, pinpoint strengths/weaknesses (methods, gaps, theoretical framework issues), formulate questions needing further research, connect topic to the broader field.

### Clustering/Grouping Approaches
- **Chronological** — group by time period (good for tracing evolution of an idea).
- **Thematic** — group by subtheme.
- **Methodological** — group by the research method used by authors (not content).

### Synthesis Techniques (explicitly recommended tools)
- **Taxonomies** — visual/hierarchical classification of approaches in the field (e.g., taxonomy of ML algorithms).
- **Tabulated Comparisons** — comparison tables summarizing lit review findings across papers/dimensions.

### Critiquing Literature — Questions to Ask
Are there better approaches/methods/models? Has the author covered relevant literature (omissions)? Is the article objective or opinion-driven? What are strengths/limitations? What are gaps and future directions?

### Systematic Literature Review (SLR)
Defined as "a scientific investigation that focuses on a specific question and uses explicit, planned methods to identify, select, assess, and summarize the findings of similar but separate studies."

## 6. Academic Writing Style

### Core Standard
Academic writing = based on facts/data (not opinion), formal vocabulary, references expert sources. **Same standard expected whether writing for examiners (student) or peer researchers (expert)** — audience/purpose differ, but rigor does not.

### Four Key Features of Academic Style
1. **Objectivity** — avoid personal pronouns (I, you, we); use passive voice, "it" as empty subject, or the topic itself as subject; adopt a neutral, evidence-based voice.
2. **Formality** — replace contractions/colloquialisms with full, formal vocabulary; avoid rhetorical questions; avoid unspecified categories ("things," "stuff").
3. **Precision** — be specific (numbers, dates); avoid vague quantifiers ("very," "a lot"); avoid imprecise multi-meaning verbs (do, make, put, get) and multi-word/phrasal verbs (cut off, find out) — replace with single precise verbs (discontinue, discover).
4. **Hedging** — use cautious language to avoid unqualified/easily-disproven claims: quantifiers (few, many, some), adverbs (occasionally, often), modal verbs (can, may, might, could). Avoid both over-confidence AND over-caution.

### Additional Rules
- No contractions ("isn't" → "is not"), no colloquialisms, no first/second person.
- Avoid abbreviations (i.e., e.g., a.k.a.) in formal prose where a full explanation is possible; avoid unspecific determinate words (super, very, incredible, huge).
- Use **signposting** language to guide the reader through the argument (e.g., "Having discussed X, a solution can now be presented...").
- Explain yourself fully — don't assume the reader shares your inference chain.
- Mix long and short sentences for rhythm; avoid over-long sentences.

### Masters-Level Expectations (Deep Learner Approach)
Demonstrate domain knowledge; apply theory to practice; analyze relevant material; evaluate theory/evidence in context; synthesize new knowledge; reflect critically on learning. A "deep learner" relates ideas to prior knowledge, sees the big picture, examines argument logic, and is interested in wider reading — as opposed to surface/rote learning.

## 7. Research Proposal & Thesis Structure (as taught in Handout 4)

> **Note:** cross-reference this against the SLIIT-specific TAF/Guidelines PDFs for the exact mandated chapter template for your cohort — this section is the *generic* structure taught as the conceptual foundation.

### Sample Project Proposal Structure
**A. Front Matter** — Title Page, Abstract, Table of Contents, List of Figures, List of Tables, Acronyms
**B. Body** — Introduction (Motivation/Rationale, Problem Statement, Significance/Potential Contribution, Aims and Objectives, Research Questions, Scope, Report Structure) → Background (Literature Review, Theoretical Foundations) → Methodology → Plan of Work (Timeline and Deliverables) → Conclusions
**C. End Matter** — References

### Sample Thesis Structure
**Front Matter** — Title Page, Abstract, Table of Contents, List of Figures, List of Tables, Acronyms
**Body** — Introduction (Motivation, Problem Statement, Significance, Aims/Objectives, Research Questions, Scope, Chapter Overview) → Background (Literature Review, Theoretical Framework) → Methodology → Results → Conclusions
**End Matter** — References, Appendices

### Section-by-Section Guidance

**Abstract** — ~150–200 words; write LAST (after the full report is done); covers Introduction, Problem Statement, Background, Research Questions, Methodology, Key Findings, Conclusions.

**Introduction** — provides context, states purpose, indicates scope, includes a chapter-overview section; should be concise; demonstrates domain mastery via currency/quality of citations.

**Statement of the Problem** — Answers: "What is the research gap that needs to be filled?" and/or "What is the problem to be solved?" State clearly and early.

**Typical Tasks in a Dissertation** (checklist):
1. Review basic concepts/theories/tools.
2. Justify significance of what is planned/achieved.
3. Review literature (history of the relevant body of knowledge).
4. Develop methodology — **what methods/tools/techniques were used and WHY** (justification linked back to Lit Review), Design, Design Assumptions, Implementation.
5. Conduct Evaluation Experiments (Pilot + Final Experiments).
6. Present and explain results.
7. Critically analyze outcomes.
8. State strengths and limitations.
9. Justify contribution of the work.

**End Matter / Appendices** — All sources must be cited (use a reference manager from the start). **Source code should NOT normally be included in the appendix**; only very important code segments may appear in the Implementation chapter if essential.

**Numbering** — Number chapters, sections, sub-sections, figures, tables, equations, appendices. Convention: `Figure 2.1` = Chapter 2, Figure 1. Use word processor auto-numbering/auto-TOC generation, not manual numbering.

**Figures & Tables** — Use auto-captioning/cross-referencing tools (not manual numbering). Caption placed *below* figures, *above* tables. Captions should be reasonably descriptive/self-contained (not just a label), often in italics.

**Equations** — Numbered, referenced in text as "(3)" or "Equation (3)" or "Eq. (x)" (spelled out if starting a sentence). Use the word processor's equation editor for auto-numbering.

**Chapter Summary** — brief recap at the end of each chapter of the main points/findings, serving as a reader's guide.

**Results** — findings/conclusions from the systematic investigation; presented via tables/figures/charts/equations + textual explanation of key points; may compare against expected/baseline outcomes.

**Conclusion (chapter)** — recall the original goal; outline theoretical background/methodology/results/analysis; highlight contribution(s) and summarize achievements; state limitations; discuss future work.
- **Conclusion ≠ Discussion**: the conclusion should be *shorter and more general*, must NOT introduce new data/interpretations/arguments.
- **4-Step process to write a conclusion:** (1) Answer the research question directly; (2) Summarize/reflect on the research process (not just repeat prior sections); (3) Make future recommendations; (4) Emphasize contributions to the field (tie back to problem statement and lit-review gap).
- **Conclusion checklist:** clearly answered main RQ; summarized key takeaways; mentioned important limitations; given relevant recommendations; explained contribution to field; introduced NO new data/arguments.

**Limitations** — be honest/objective (don't minimize or exaggerate); identify specific limitations (sample size, selection bias, measurement error); provide context for why the limitation occurred; discuss implications for validity/generalizability; suggest future research addressing them.

### Research Contribution — How to Write It (6-step process taught)
1. Define the research problem/question and the gap it addresses.
2. Conduct a thorough literature review identifying the knowledge gap.
3. Describe research design/methodology (methods, procedures, analysis techniques used).
4. Present findings clearly (with statistical/visual support).
5. Discuss implications (significance, recommendations, applications).
6. Conclude by summarizing the contribution and restating significance.

Where to place it: typically **after** literature review, methodology, results, and analysis sections — but articulated also in abstract, introduction, and conclusion.

### Scope of the Research
Purpose: defines study boundaries; determines objectives/RQs to address; provides direction/focus; identifies relevant theories/variables; enables methodology selection; allows resource allocation; establishes sample/data-collection criteria; ensures ethical considerations addressed. Key advice: define an "**effort budget**" — do not begin work without a clear idea of what is to be accomplished.

## 8. Ethics in Research

### Historical/Foundational Frameworks (explicitly taught — know these)

**The Nuremberg Code (1947)** — 10 principles arising from post-WWII Nazi medical experiment trials. Key points: voluntary informed consent is absolutely essential; experiment must yield results unobtainable by other means; must avoid unnecessary suffering/injury; no experiment should proceed if death/injury is anticipated (except where researchers are also subjects); risk must not exceed the humanitarian importance of the problem; conducted only by qualified persons; subject can end participation at any time; researcher must be prepared to terminate if harm becomes likely.

**The Belmont Report** — 3 core ethical principles (used as the basis for later AI ethics frameworks too):
| Principle | Meaning | Practice |
|---|---|---|
| **Respect for Persons** | Each person has individual rights | Obtain informed consent; protect privacy; maintain confidentiality |
| **Beneficence** | Provide benefit, protect from harm, limit risk | Risk-benefit assessment; standard procedures |
| **Justice** | Equitable selection of subjects | Include all groups that may benefit; don't single out one group. 5 distribution methods: equal share, individual need, individual effort, societal contribution, merit |

### Ethics vs Law
Ethics = moral principles determining right/wrong (not universally agreed). Law = codified rules created by an authority to maintain social order. They can diverge.

### Research Misconduct — Formal Definitions (FFP)
- **(a) Fabrication** — making up data/results.
- **(b) Falsification** — manipulating materials/equipment/processes, or changing/omitting data so the record misrepresents the research.
- **(c) Plagiarism** — appropriating another's ideas/processes/results/words without credit.
> Note: research misconduct explicitly does NOT include honest error or differences of opinion.

### Other Ethical Issue Categories (10 areas listed)
1. Research misconduct (FFP)
2. Collaboration issues (authorship, data ownership)
3. Peer review integrity
4. Conflicts of interest/obligation
5. Complicity and funding sources
6. Animal subject research
7. Human subject research
8. Redundant publication
9. Misuse of privacy-related information
10. **Salami Slicing** — breaking a large study into multiple smaller "sliced" publications (distinct from duplicate publication, which republishes identical data).

### 16 General Principles of Research Ethics (explicit numbered list taught — comprehensive)
1. **Honesty** — report data/methods/results honestly; no fabrication/falsification.
2. **Integrity** — keep promises/agreements; consistency of thought and action.
3. **Objectivity** — avoid bias in design, analysis, interpretation, peer review.
4. **Informed Consent** — participant knowingly/voluntarily/intelligently consents; informed of objective, role, benefits/harms.
5. **Respect for Person/Respondent** — autonomy + protection of vulnerable/dependent persons.
6. **Beneficence** — maximize benefits to participants, minimize harm.
7. **Non-maleficence** — "do no harm"; ensure privacy, autonomy, dignity.
8. **Responsible Publication** — publish to advance the field, not just career; avoid duplicate/wasteful publication.
9. **Protecting Anonymity** — do not reveal identity-linked information.
10. **Confidentiality** — protect submitted papers, personnel records, patient records, etc.
11. **Non-discrimination** — avoid discrimination unrelated to the study (age, sex, race, etc.).
12. **Openness** — be willing to share data/results/tools; accept constructive criticism.
13. **Carefulness & Respect for IP** — honor patents/copyrights; don't use unpublished data/methods without permission; keep good records.
14. **Justice** — fair distribution of benefits/burdens; equal treatment.
15. **Legality** — know and obey relevant laws/institutional policies.
16. **Competence** — maintain/improve professional expertise through lifelong learning (linked explicitly to the BCS Code of Conduct).

### Types of Possible Harm to Human Subjects
Physical, Psychological, Social, Economic, Legal.

### Vulnerable Subject Categories
Children; Minority groups; Mentally impaired persons; Prisoners; Individuals with medical conditions; Individuals with special needs.

### Authorship — CRediT Taxonomy (14 Contributor Roles)
Used to represent each contributor's specific role in a research output: Conceptualization, Data curation, Formal Analysis, Funding acquisition, Investigation, Methodology, Project administration, Resources, Software, Supervision, Validation, Visualization, Writing–original draft, Writing–review & editing. (Reference: https://credit.niso.org/)

### AI Ethics (extends Belmont Report into AI/ML context — explicitly taught)
Belmont's 3 principles reframed for algorithm/AI design:
1. **Respect for Persons** → autonomy, informed consent, right to withdraw from AI-driven experiments.
2. **Beneficence** → "do no harm" applied to algorithms; guard against bias amplification.
3. **Justice** → fairness in who bears the benefits/burdens of AI/ML systems.

**Primary AI ethics concern areas taught:** foundation models/generative AI (bias, false content, lack of explainability); technological singularity; AI's impact on jobs; privacy (GDPR 2016, CCPA); bias and discrimination (e.g., Amazon's biased hiring algorithm case); accountability (lack of universal AI legislation).

**Governance component of AI ethics:** define roles/responsibilities; educate all AI-lifecycle participants; establish build/manage/monitor processes; leverage tools for trustworthiness. An "AI Ethics Board" is cited as an effective governance mechanism.

### Ethical Clearance Process
The notes reference an example ethical clearance flowchart/process (source: University of Cambridge POLIS) and point students to the University of Uva Wellassa's **"Guidelines for Ethical Clearance of Research"** as a worked example. General shape of an ethical clearance process (as implied across the notes): researcher submits proposal to an Ethics Review Committee / Institutional Review Board (IRB) → committee reviews for protection of rights, dignity, welfare of respondents → approval/rejection/revision request → research may proceed only after clearance, especially where human subjects, PII, or vulnerable groups are involved.

**Practically for IT4010 students:** any project involving human subjects (surveys, interviews, usability testing, data collection from people) should include: informed consent forms, explanation of study goals/procedures without bias, guarantee of confidentiality/anonymity, right to withdraw at any time, and — per the Usability Testing section (Handout 8) — ethical issues must be considered explicitly at the study-design stage (see Section 10 below, "DECIDE" framework).

## 9. Professional Codes of Conduct & Intellectual Property

### Professional Bodies Referenced
- **BCS (British Computer Society)** Code of Conduct
- **IET** (Institution of Engineering and Technology)
- **ACM** Code of Ethics and Professional Conduct
- **IEEE** Code of Ethics / Code of Conduct
- **ACM/IEEE-CS Joint Task Force** — Software Engineering Code of Ethics and Professional Practice (SEEPP)
- **CSSL** (Computer Society of Sri Lanka) Code of Ethics

### BCS Code of Conduct — 4 Pillars
1. **Public Interest** — regard for public health/privacy/security/wellbeing/environment; no discrimination; promote equal access to IT.
2. **Professional Competence and Integrity** — only undertake work within competence; don't claim false competence; continuously develop skills; comply with legislation; respect alternative viewpoints; reject bribery.
3. **Duty to Relevant Authority** — due care/diligence; avoid conflicts of interest; accept responsibility for own/supervised work; protect confidential information.
4. **Duty to the Profession** — uphold reputation; improve professional standards; act with integrity toward peers.

### IEEE Code of Conduct — 5 Rules
1. Be respectful of others (incl. privacy).
2. Treat people fairly (no harassment/discrimination).
3. Avoid injuring others/property/reputation/employment.
4. Refrain from retaliation against those reporting misconduct.
5. Comply with applicable laws (no bribery, avoid conflicts of interest, protect confidential info, no price-fixing, respect others' IP).

### Software Engineering Code of Ethics (ACM/IEEE-CS, Version 5.2) — 8 Principles
1. **Public** — act consistent with public interest.
2. **Client and Employer** — act in their best interests, consistent with public interest.
3. **Product** — ensure products/modifications meet highest professional standards (includes realistic cost/scheduling estimates, adequate testing/documentation, privacy-respecting design).
4. **Judgment** — maintain integrity/independence of professional judgment; disclose conflicts of interest.
5. **Management** — ethical management of software development (fair remuneration, due process, don't punish ethical concerns).
6. **Profession** — advance integrity/reputation of the profession.
7. **Colleagues** — be fair/supportive; credit others' work fully.
8. **Self** — commit to lifelong learning and ethical practice.
(Each principle has ~6–15 detailed sub-clauses in the original — see https://ethics.acm.org/code-of-ethics/software-engineering-code/ for the full text if needed.)

### Intellectual Property
- **Patents** — jurisdictional (no single "world patent"); international protection via the **Patent Cooperation Treaty (PCT)** (single application, multiple countries) or the **Paris Convention** (file individually per country). To be patentable: must be **original**, **non-obvious**, and **useful**. US patent term: 20 years.
- **Trademark** — word/phrase/logo/shape/sound identifying a source of goods/services; protects consumers. Common law trademark = "™"; registered trademark = "®".
- **Copyright** relevant UK legislation referenced: Copyright, Designs and Patents Act 1988; Data Protection Act 1998/2018; Computer Misuse Act 1990. Sri Lankan context: Computer Crime Act.

### Data Privacy Concepts
- **PII (Personally Identifiable Information)** — name, NIC/SSN, DOB, biometric records, medical/educational/financial/employment info; organizations collecting PII are responsible for protecting it.
- **5-principle data security plan** (FTC): Take Stock (know what PII you hold), Scale Down (keep only what's needed), Lock It (protect what you keep), Pitch It (dispose properly), Plan Ahead (incident response plan).
- **NORA (Non-Obvious Relationship Awareness)** — data mining technique aggregating disparate data sources/types to discover non-obvious relationships between identities; raises privacy concerns even when individual data points aren't PII.
- **GDPR** (EU, May 2016) — individual data privacy rights supersede business interests; restricts cross-border data transfer.
- **Do Not Track (DNT)**: US uses an "**opt-out**" model (default = shared, must explicitly refuse); EU uses an "**opt-in**" model (default = not shared, must explicitly consent).

## 10. Referencing / Citation Style (Critical — Answers "What Citation Style Must We Use")

### Primary Style Taught: **Harvard Referencing** (specifically the "Cite Them Right" variant is mentioned as one common version)
The course dedicates an entire handout (Handout 7) almost exclusively to **Harvard style** mechanics:
- **In-text citation format:** Author surname + year in parentheses, e.g., `(Dostoyevsky, 2004, p. 5)`. Use `p.` for one page, `pp.` for a range.
- **Parenthetical citation:** `(Schofield & Germov, 2019)` — appears within/at end of sentence.
- **Narrative citation:** `Schofield and Germov (2019) state that...` — author named as part of the sentence.
- **Multiple authors:** cite all authors up to 3; for 4+, use first author + "et al."
- **No page number available** (e.g., websites): omit, or use a locator like `(Scribbr, para. 4)`.
- **Multiple sources at one point:** separate with semicolons, ordered by publication date: `(Singh, 2011; Davidson, 2015; Harding, 2018)`.
- **Same author, same year, multiple works:** disambiguate with a/b/c suffix: `(Woodhouse, 2018a)`, `(Woodhouse, 2018b)`.
- **No author:** use the organization as corporate author, or the title of the source if no corporate author exists.
- **No date:** use "no date"; still include an access date for online sources.
- **Reference list:** alphabetical by author surname, full details for reader lookup; author surname + initials; only first word of title (and proper nouns) capitalized.
- **Note:** Wikis and blogs should NOT appear in an academic thesis's reference list.

### Important Nuance — IEEE Style Also Explicitly Referenced
Tutorial 2 (Handout 2) instructs students to "Learn how to correctly use a referencing style **IEEE/Harvard** Ref Style" — meaning **both IEEE and Harvard styles are acceptable/taught**, and students should check with their specific supervisor/department (via the TAF/Guidelines PDFs) on which is mandated for the actual final thesis submission. **The lecture notes themselves teach Harvard mechanics in the greatest depth**, but explicitly acknowledge IEEE as an alternative valid style — students must confirm the department's mandated style from the TAF Guidelines document, not assume Harvard applies by default.

### Reference Management Tool: Zotero (heavily emphasized, with a full practical tutorial)
- Free, open-source; collects/organizes/cites sources; can auto-import from library catalogs/databases/web pages.
- **MS Word integration**: Zotero plugin installed automatically; use the Zotero tab in Word to insert in-text citations and auto-generate the bibliography — switch citation style anytime via Zotero preferences (Edit → Preferences/Settings → Cite → Styles).
- Also works with **Google Docs** and **Overleaf** (LaTeX).
- Supports PDF annotation (highlight, underline, notes, area-select, draw) directly inside Zotero, plus screenshot/webpage-snapshot saving.
- Other reference tools mentioned: **Mendeley**, **EndNote**.
- Other plagiarism-detection tools mentioned: **Turnitin**, **Scribbr**, **Viper**.
> **Note on similarity thresholds:** the lecture notes do NOT specify a numeric Turnitin similarity percentage threshold anywhere in the 525 pages — that number (if mandated, e.g. a specific % cap) will be in the SLIIT-specific TAF/Guidelines PDFs, not in this teaching material. Do not assume a percentage from this document.

### Why Cite (rationale taught)
Avoids plagiarism (credits original author); allows readers to verify claims/do follow-up research; shows engagement with the field's literature; establishes credibility for novelty claims; frames the significance/rationale of your research questions.

### Peer Review — What It Actually Certifies
Peer review checks that methodology is appropriate and properly applied, other relevant work is accounted for, and conclusions are adequately supported. **Peer review does NOT guarantee 100% correctness** — it is a "stamp of approval" that experts judged the work a worthy contribution, not a certification of absolute truth.

## 11. Evaluation & Validation Methodologies (Handout 8 — Results and Evaluation)

This is the most detailed methodological section beyond generic proposal-writing, and answers "how do we validate our system/model" style questions.

### Framing Questions for a Results/Evaluation Chapter
- What is the evaluation protocol/methodology (check how similar work in your domain was evaluated — tie back to Lit Review)?
- What results-analysis methods will be used?
- How will results be presented?
- How does the proposed approach compare positionally with alternatives?
- Choose evaluation **criteria** up front: time/resource efficiency, coverage, effectiveness, user-friendliness, etc.
- Experiments/comparisons must be **repeatable**, and the baselines you compare against **must already have been discussed in the Lit Review/Background chapter** (don't introduce a new comparison baseline for the first time in the results chapter).
- Be honest/transparent — apply the same academic-writing rigor as elsewhere.

### Terminology: POC vs Prototype
- **Proof of Concept (POC)** — an initial test of an idea/method/product to show potential/feasibility.
- **Prototype** (paper, low-fidelity, or high-fidelity) — an actual working model showing mechanics/design/UX; a scaled-down testable version of the final product.

### Validation Methods for Computing Research (general menu)
- **Simulation** — predict system behavior via an approximate model.
- **Emulation** — run the application on a model of the environment.
- **Benchmarking** — compare against existing benchmarks (common in ML projects).
- **Usability Evaluation Experiments** — for HCI/UX work.

### Types of Evaluation (by project type)
1. Quantitative evaluation/experiments of algorithms, models.
2. Usability evaluations.
3. Questionnaires/surveys.
4. Case studies.
5. Simulation/Emulation.
6. ML model evaluations.
7. GenAI model evaluations.

### ML Model Testing — Types (explicitly taught, directly useful for ML-based final year projects)
- **Unit Testing** — test individual pipeline components (preprocessing, feature extraction, architecture, hyperparameters).
- **Data Testing and Preprocessing** — verify data integrity/accuracy/consistency; validate normalization/cleaning.
- **Cross-Validation** — partition data into subsets, train/test across them, average results to assess generalization.
- **Performance Metrics Testing** — accuracy, precision, recall, F1-score.
- **Robustness/Adversarial Testing** — behavior under unexpected/adversarial inputs.
- **A/B Testing** — compare new model vs existing model in a real-world/production-like setting.
- **Bias Testing** — check for unfair/discriminatory outcomes across groups.

### ML Evaluation Metrics — Definitions (explicitly taught)
- **Accuracy** — ratio of correct predictions to total; weak for imbalanced datasets.
- **Precision** — ratio of true positives to (true positives + false positives); matters when false positives are costly.
- **Sensitivity/Recall (true positive rate)** — ratio of true positives to (true positives + false negatives); matters when false negatives are costly.
- **Specificity (true negative rate)** — ratio of true negatives to (true negatives + false positives).
- **AUC-ROC** — plots true positive rate vs false positive rate for binary classification; closer to 1 = better.
- **MAE (Mean Absolute Error)** — average absolute difference between predicted and actual (regression).
- **RMSE (Root Mean Squared Error)** — square root of average squared differences; penalizes large errors more heavily (regression).

### Evaluating Generative AI Models (LLMs / chatbots — directly relevant to GenAI-based FYPs)
**Objectives to define before evaluating** (context-dependent): engagingness, factual accuracy (hallucination check), personalization, privacy/security, multilingual support.

**Quality dimensions** (per Clarivate framework cited): Relevance (does the response address the query), Accuracy/Faithfulness (does source material support the answer — hallucination check), Clarity and structure, Bias/offensive content, Comprehensiveness (multiple perspectives considered), Behavior when information is lacking (does it acknowledge uncertainty rather than fabricate — "noise reduction/negative rejection").

**Testing methods:**
- **Manual review** — essential early in development; surfaces subtle issues.
- **Semi-automated testing** — workflows simulating real-world usage as the system matures; test answer consistency, response quality across content types/languages, alignment with expected behaviors.
- **LLM-evaluating-LLM** — one model generates, a second evaluates against criteria; scalable but risks shared blind spots — human oversight still needed for high-stakes cases.
- **RAGAS (Retrieval-Augmented Generation Assessment)** — evaluates relevance, context, and faithfulness of RAG-based answers specifically.

### Usability Evaluation (HCI / UX-focused projects)
**Usability Metrics:** success rate, task completion time, error rate, subjective satisfaction.

**Study Design Choice:**
- **Between-Subjects** — different people test each condition (each person sees only one interface).
- **Within-Subjects (repeated measures)** — same person tests all conditions.
- **Independent variables** (manipulated by researcher) vs **Dependent variables** (measured outcomes).

**Formative vs Summative Evaluation:**
- **Formative** — iterative, used to improve before production/release.
- **Summative** — evaluates a finished/shipped product against a benchmark.

**Three Main Types of Usability Evaluation:**
1. **Usability Testing** — observing real users perform tasks.
2. **Usability Inquiry** — talking to users about expectations/needs.
3. **Usability Inspection** — expert assessment (heuristic evaluation, cognitive walkthrough) without real users.

**Usability Evaluation measures 3 dimensions:** Effectiveness (accuracy of goal achievement), Efficiency (resources used), Satisfaction (comfort/pleasantness).

**Evaluation Paradigms:** Quick-and-dirty (informal discussion), Usability Testing (observe + record), Field Studies (go to user sites), Predictive (expert heuristic/formal-model evaluation without users).

**DECIDE Framework for Planning a Usability Test** (explicit 6-step framework — useful checklist):
1. **D**etermine the general goals of the test.
2. **E**xplore the specific questions.
3. **C**hoose the evaluation paradigms/techniques.
4. **I**dentify practical issues (participant selection, tasks).
5. **D**ecide how to handle ethical issues.
6. **E**valuate, interpret, and present the data.

**Designing and Conducting a User Test — 10-Step Process:**
1. Identify purpose and metrics. 2. Decide data type to collect. 3. Design tasks. 4. Develop experimental design. 5. Develop detailed plan/artifacts/protocol/scripts. 6. **Ethical approval.** 7. Pilot test and revise. 8. Recruit participants and run study. 9. Analyze data. 10. Report results.

**Pilot Testing** — run a session or two before the real test to fine-tune wording/timing; can also supply an extra data point.

**Ethical Issues Specific to Usability Testing:**
- Explain test goals without biasing results (e.g., say "we want to learn how this UI works for you," not "we want to see if you miss a button").
- Explain tasks without bias.
- Promise confidentiality and anonymity.
- Tell participants they may stop at any time.
- Pay participants when possible (keeps the relationship professional).
- Avoid identifying quotes in the report unless the participant explicitly consents to being quoted.

**Evaluating/Interpreting Results — 5 Considerations:** Reliability (repeatable?), Validity (measures what it claims to?), Biases (acknowledged?), Scope of test (can't test everything), Ecological validity (environmental factors that might bias results).

**Question Design Pitfalls (bad questions):** too long, confusing, overly elaborate sentence construction, jargon/technical terms, leading questions.

**Standard Survey Question Types:** Yes/No/Maybe; Likert scales; Agree/disagree scales; Frequency of use; Semantic differential scales; Checkbox options; Comparison questions; Ranking; Short answers.

**Interview Types:** Open-ended, Unstructured, Structured, Semi-structured, Group. (Open-ended and group interviews require more skill and are harder to analyze — structured interviews are easier to run/analyze for less experienced researchers.)

**Observation/Recording Techniques:** Notes, audio recording, still photographs, video, event-logging software.

**Standard Usability Test Session Structure:** Prepare room → Greet + consent form → Pre-test questionnaire (demographics) → Explain interface → Present scenario/task → Post-scenario questionnaire/interview → Repeat for each scenario → Post-test questionnaire → Thank participant → Organize files.

**Expert Evaluation Methods (no real users needed):** Cognitive Walkthroughs, Heuristic Evaluations.

### Good Research/Experiment Principles (repeated emphasis)
Good research is **replicable, reproducible, and transparent**. Experiments are time-consuming and must be carried out meticulously; comparisons must be fair and transparent.

### How to Write a Results Section (best practices)
- Be as concise as possible.
- Include only results directly relevant to the research questions (move extras to appendix/footnotes).
- Start broad, then move to granular findings (the "shoe shop" analogy: discuss shoes as a whole, then trainers/boots/sandals specifically).

## 13. Quick-Reference Answers to Likely Future Questions

**Q: What citation style must we use?**
A: The course teaches **Harvard style** in depth (specifically referencing the "Cite Them Right" variant as one common implementation), but Tutorial 2 explicitly also names **IEEE** as an acceptable style ("IEEE/Harvard Ref Style"). **Confirm the actual mandated style against the department's TAF/Guidelines documents** — this lecture material teaches the mechanics of Harvard citation most thoroughly but does not itself mandate one style over the other for the final thesis.

**Q: How many sources/papers are required in our literature review?**
A: The lecture notes do not give an explicit minimum *paper count*. They do give a **word-count benchmark: a full literature review is typically 6,000–12,000 words** (vs. ~150 words/source for an annotated bibliography). Check the TAF/Guidelines PDFs for any explicit minimum source count mandated by SLIIT for your specific submission.

**Q: What is the required research methodology structure/approach?**
A: There is no single mandated methodology — the course teaches a broad toolbox (qualitative/quantitative/mixed, experimental, case study, action research, design science, constructive research, etc.) and expects the student to **justify their chosen methodology with supporting literature**, acknowledge its limitations, and provide rationale for the choices made (see Section 7, "Methodology" guidance). The methodology chapter must explain what methods/tools/techniques were used and **why**, linked back to the literature review.

**Q: How do we identify and justify our research gap?**
A: Use the course's 5-type taxonomy — **Theoretical, Empirical, Methodological, Practical, or Knowledge gap** — and follow the 7-step identification process in Section 4. The gap must be derived from (and justified by) the literature review, not asserted independently.

**Q: Is there a Turnitin/plagiarism similarity threshold?**
A: Not specified anywhere in this lecture-notes PDF. Turnitin is named as the recommended plagiarism-detection tool, but no numeric similarity-percentage cap is given in this source — check the TAF/Guidelines PDFs for that number.

**Q: What ethics process must we follow if our project involves human subjects/data?**
A: Apply the Belmont Report's 3 principles (Respect for Persons, Beneficence, Justice), obtain informed consent, ensure confidentiality/anonymity, allow withdrawal at any time, and seek Ethics Review Committee/IRB clearance before data collection. For usability studies specifically, follow the **DECIDE framework** and explicitly plan for ethical issues (bias-free framing, confidentiality, right to stop, fair compensation).

**Q: How should we evaluate/validate our system (e.g., an ML model or app)?**
A: Depends on project type — see Section 11. For ML models: use standard metrics (accuracy, precision, recall/sensitivity, specificity, AUC-ROC, MAE/RMSE) plus cross-validation, bias testing, and robustness testing. For GenAI/LLM-based systems: define quality dimensions (relevance, faithfulness/hallucination, clarity, bias, comprehensiveness) and use manual + semi-automated testing, potentially LLM-as-judge or RAGAS. For usability/HCI-focused systems: use the DECIDE framework, formative/summative testing, and standard usability metrics (success rate, time-on-task, error rate, satisfaction). All approaches must be **repeatable** and compared against baselines already established in the Literature Review chapter.

**Q: What's the required thesis chapter structure?**
A: Front Matter (Title, Abstract, TOC, Figures/Tables lists, Acronyms) → Introduction (motivation, problem statement, significance, aims/objectives, RQs, scope, chapter overview) → Background/Literature Review (+ Theoretical Framework if applicable) → Methodology → Results → Conclusions → References → Appendices. (Cross-check exact chapter titles/order against the SLIIT TAF/Guidelines PDFs, since this is the generic structure taught conceptually, not necessarily SLIIT's mandated template.)
